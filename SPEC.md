# Forecast governance layer for a component supplier — build spec

## Purpose

A small, honest reference implementation of a **model-agnostic forecast governance layer** for demand planning at an industrial component supplier whose customers are capital-equipment OEMs. The point is not a better forecasting model. The point is to show what every demand forecast should report before a planner acts on it, and to make that visible on one page.

The example domain: a supplier with a few hundred products and a handful of large OEM customers, long lead times, lumpy order patterns, and customers who send their own rolling forecasts that are systematically biased. Partway through the history, demand steps up sharply (an industry upcycle) and history stops being a reliable guide.

**All data is synthetic and generated by code in this repo.** No real company, customer, product or figure appears anywhere. Do not name any company, real or inferred, anywhere in the repo, including comments, commit messages, file names and the README.

## Non-goals (do not build these)

- No deep learning, no foundation models, no hyperparameter search.
- No web app, no Streamlit, no dashboard framework. Static charts (PNG) and one markdown report are the output.
- No database, no cloud deployment, no Azure code. The Azure architecture is a separate document the repo owner writes by hand; leave a placeholder.
- No more than ~1,500 lines of Python in total. If it is getting bigger, you are building the wrong thing.

## 1. Synthetic panel (`synth.py`)

Monthly data, 48 months of history plus a 12-month holdout. Grain: customer × product family.

- **Customers**: 5 OEM customers, labelled C1–C5. Each has a share of total demand and a **forecast behaviour** that is the heart of the example:
  - C1: reliable — small, unbiased forecast error.
  - C2: optimistic — over-forecasts by ~30% at 6-month horizon, ~15% at 3-month; bias shrinks as horizon shortens.
  - C3: pessimistic — under-forecasts by ~15%, stable.
  - C4: volatile — unbiased on average but large month-to-month revisions (noisy signal).
  - C5: regime-sensitive — accurate in normal times, strongly over-forecasts during the upcycle (panic ordering).
- **Product families**: 12, labelled F01–F12. Mix of: 6 smooth-ish families, 4 intermittent (many zero months, order sizes lumpy), 2 that are new in the last 12 months of history (short history).
- **Demand process**: base level per customer × family, mild seasonality, lognormal noise; intermittent families drawn as occurrence (Bernoulli) × size (lognormal).
- **The step change**: at month 37 of 48, total demand rises by a factor drawn from 1.8–2.2, ramping over 3 months, then plateaus at the new level. Not every family steps equally (range 1.3×–2.8×). Long-lead families see the step first.
- **Customer forecasts**: for each month and each customer × family, generate the customer's forecast of that month made 1, 3 and 6 months earlier, by applying the customer's bias and noise profile to the true demand. Store as a long table with columns `origin_month, target_month, horizon, customer, family, customer_forecast`.
- **Order book**: for each month, the confirmed orders already on the book for the next 1–6 months (a fraction of true demand that grows as the month approaches: ~30% at 6 months, ~85% at 1 month, higher during the upcycle because customers order earlier). Columns `as_of_month, target_month, confirmed_qty`.

Outputs: `actuals.parquet` (or csv), `customer_forecasts.csv`, `order_book.csv`. Document every parameter at the top of the file in a single dataclass so the owner can change the story.

## 2. Candidate forecasts (`forecast.py`)

All produced with **rolling-origin backtesting**: origins at each of the last 24 months of history plus the 12 holdout months; horizons 1, 3 and 6. Every method produces point forecasts and, where cheap, prediction intervals (use statsforecast conformal intervals; otherwise empirical residual quantiles).

1. **Seasonal naive** — the floor every method must beat.
2. **Statistical baseline** — statsforecast `AutoETS` for smooth families, `TSB` for intermittent families (classify by zero share > 40% in the training window). History only.
3. **Customer forecast, raw** — the customer's own number for that target and horizon.
4. **Customer forecast, bias-corrected** — divide the raw forecast by that customer's trailing bias ratio (sum of forecasts / sum of actuals over the last 12 realised months at that horizon), computed only from information available at the origin. This is the method the example is about.
5. **Order-book-anchored** — confirmed quantity divided by the trailing fill ratio for that horizon (same trailing-12 logic).
6. **Blend** — simple average of 2, 4 and 5, with weights chosen per family by trailing FVA (no optimiser; pick the best single method per family over the previous 12 origins, with blend as a candidate).

Keep each method under ~60 lines. No feature engineering beyond what is listed.

## 3. Metrics (`metrics.py`)

Computed per method, per horizon, overall and by customer and by family. Scale-free, always paired with bias.

- **MASE** against seasonal naive (in-sample scaling as in Hyndman).
- **Bias**: mean (forecast − actual) / mean actual, signed. Report by customer; this is the headline number for the customer-forecast story.
- **FVA**: MASE improvement of each method versus seasonal naive, and versus method 2 (the stat baseline), in percentage points. Report as a multi-origin average, not per origin.
- **Stability**: mean absolute revision of the forecast for the same target month between consecutive origins, scaled by mean actual.
- **Interval coverage** at 80%: share of actuals inside the interval, versus the 80% target.
- **Pre/post break split**: every metric reported for origins before month 37 and after, so the step change is visible in the numbers.

## 4. Break detection (`breaks.py`)

- Run `ruptures` PELT (RBF kernel) on the total monthly demand series and on each family, at each origin using only data up to that origin (so the detection date is honest, not hindsight).
- Report the first origin at which a break at or after month 37 is detected, per family. Expected: detected within 2–4 months of onset.
- Emit an **alert table**: `family, break_detected_at_origin, months_after_onset, pre_break_level, post_break_level`.
- When a break is active for a family, methods that use history (1, 2) are flagged `history_unreliable = True` in the planner table; the blend weight logic (method 6) restricts to the trailing 6 origins instead of 12.

## 5. Report (`report.py`)

Produce `outputs/governance_report.md` and `outputs/charts/`. The report is for a planner and their manager, not a data scientist.

**Charts (PNG, matplotlib, no seaborn, consistent muted palette, labelled axes):**

1. `total_demand_and_break.png` — total monthly demand with the detected break marked and the detection lag annotated.
2. `customer_bias_by_horizon.png` — bias per customer at horizons 1, 3, 6: raw customer forecast vs bias-corrected. One grouped bar chart.
3. `fva_by_method.png` — FVA versus seasonal naive, by method, pre-break and post-break side by side.
4. `tracking_c5.png` — for customer C5 one family: actual, stat baseline, raw customer forecast, bias-corrected, through the step change. This is the one chart that tells the story.
5. `stability_vs_accuracy.png` — scatter of methods, x = MASE, y = stability.

**Planner table (`planner_table.csv`, and rendered in the report):** one row per customer × family for the next planning month, columns: `recommended_source` (which method won on trailing FVA), `forecast`, `lo80`, `hi80`, `customer_bias_trailing`, `history_unreliable`, `attention_flag` (true when bias > 20% or when the recommended source changed since last origin or when a break is newly detected). Sorted so attention flags come first.

**Report sections, in this order, each starting with its one-sentence finding:**
1. What this is (three sentences; say synthetic data in the first).
2. Which customer forecasts to trust, with chart 2 and the bias numbers.
3. What happened at the break, with charts 1 and 4 and the detection lag.
4. Which forecast source to use where, with chart 3 and the planner table.
5. Stability, with chart 5, and why planners should care.
6. What a forecast must report before a planner sees it — a short list that references `docs/governance_standard.md` without duplicating it.
7. Limitations: synthetic data, no price/promo effects, no capacity constraints, five customers.

## 6. Tests

- Synth: shapes, no negatives, zero share of intermittent families in range, step factor applied, customer bias recovered within tolerance from the generated forecasts.
- Metrics: MASE of seasonal naive against itself is 1; bias sign correct on a constructed case; FVA zero for identical forecasts.
- Breaks: synthetic step detected within 4 origins of onset in at least 10 of 12 families.
- Run: `python -m fgov.run` completes and writes all listed outputs.
